Cultural essay · Beijing

An unsurpassed night of glory

The ambition, symbolism and international image of the 2008 Summer Olympics opening ceremony.

The opening ceremony of the 2008 Summer Olympics, held on 8 August 2008 at Beijing National Stadium, is—in my opinion—among the most ambitious, meticulously choreographed and symbolically dense ceremonies in Olympic history.

Directed by the acclaimed Chinese filmmaker Zhang Yimou, the event functioned not only as a celebration of sport but as a grand presentation to the world of Chinese civilisation, identity and modern aspirations.

China had wanted to host the Olympic Games for quite some time. Beijing came frustratingly close to securing the 2000 Games, leading every round of voting before losing to Sydney by two votes in the final round.

Its desire to host the 2008 Games was driven by political as well as symbolic objectives. Hosting the Olympics is one of the most visible ways a country can assert global status—or at least that is the perception the IOC seeks to cultivate. The central idea was for China to present itself as a modern, confident and influential global power. Every facet of the bid, and indeed the opening ceremony, was intended to showcase the country for everything it had been and what it expected to become.

Few in 2008 could have anticipated the civilisational presentation that followed, or its extraordinary scale. The ceremony mobilised thousands of performers inside the Bird’s Nest stadium and reached an enormous audience around the world.

For my part, I remember quite clearly watching the entire ceremony from my living room in Gandía, Spain.

The four-hour spectacle depicted an immensely proud nation that had made monumental contributions to humanity and sought unity and harmony with nature and other nations as it continued its rise on the world stage. It was a remarkably complete presentation of the image China sought to project in international affairs.

The ceremony focused heavily on the glories of China’s ancient past while largely omitting the more difficult and contested dimensions of its recent history. That selectivity is precisely what makes it such a revealing cultural and political artefact: it was at once a work of extraordinary theatre, a personal memory and a carefully constructed national narrative.
